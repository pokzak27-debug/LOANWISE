How to use this calculator

  1. Enter the loan amount — Start with a figure you’re considering (e.g. $25,000). You can adjust this to see what payment different amounts produce.
  2. Enter the interest rate — Use a rate you expect based on your credit (e.g. 6–8% for good credit, 10–15% for average).
  3. Enter the term — 48 or 60 months are common. Shorter terms mean higher payments but less interest.
  4. Click Calculate — See the monthly payment. If it’s more than 15% of your take-home pay, try a lower amount or longer term.

Real-world example

If you bring home $4,000 per month and aim to keep your car payment under 15%, that’s $600/month. A $28,000 loan at 7% for 60 months gives a payment of about $554—within budget. At 10% the same loan would be $595. Use the calculator to find the loan amount that fits your income and target payment.

What affects how much car loan you can afford

  • Take-home pay — A common guideline is to keep the car payment at 15% or less of monthly take-home.
  • Existing debt — Student loans, credit cards, and other payments reduce what you can comfortably allocate to a car.
  • Interest rate — Better credit often means a lower rate and a lower payment for the same loan amount.
  • Total cost of ownership — Budget for insurance, fuel, and maintenance on top of the loan payment.

Simple rules to estimate what you can afford

There is no single answer—affordability depends on your income, existing debt, and how much of your monthly budget you are comfortable putting toward a car. Two common guidelines help narrow it down.

The 20% rule. Some advisers suggest limiting total vehicle costs (payment, insurance, fuel, maintenance) to 20% or less of your take-home pay. If you bring home $4,000 a month, that’s up to $800 for everything car-related. A $400 payment leaves $400 for insurance, fuel, and upkeep.

The 15% rule. Others prefer to cap the loan payment itself at 15% of take-home pay. On $4,000, that’s $600 per month. Use the calculator above to find the loan amount and term that land you near that payment.

Don’t forget the full cost of ownership

Your monthly payment is only part of the picture. Insurance, especially full coverage on a financed car, can add hundreds. Fuel and routine maintenance add more. If you stretch the loan to lower the payment, you may pay more interest over time and owe more than the car is worth for longer.

Run several scenarios: a $20,000 loan at 48 months vs 60 months, or the same payment at 5% vs 8% interest. See how total interest changes. Then pick a payment that fits your budget with room for other expenses.

Pre-approval and down payments

Getting pre-approved from a bank or credit union tells you the maximum amount and rate you qualify for. That’s useful, but you don’t have to borrow the full amount. A larger down payment reduces the loan size and often improves the rate. Use the calculator with different principal amounts to see how a bigger down payment affects the monthly payment.

Frequently asked questions

How much car loan can I afford on $50,000 salary?
It depends on take-home pay, debt, and comfort level. A rough guide: if 15% of take-home goes to the payment, and you bring home around $3,500 after tax, that’s about $525 per month. Use the calculator to find the loan amount that gives you that payment at your likely rate and term.
Should I use the dealer’s financing or my bank?
Compare both. Get a pre-approval from your bank, then see if the dealer can beat it. Sometimes manufacturer offers (including 0%) beat bank rates. Run the numbers in this calculator with each rate to compare total cost.
What if my payment feels too high?
Consider a longer term (higher total interest but lower payment), a smaller loan (cheaper car or bigger down payment), or waiting until you have more saved.
Does this calculator include insurance?
No. It only shows principal and interest. Add insurance, fuel, and maintenance when you budget.
